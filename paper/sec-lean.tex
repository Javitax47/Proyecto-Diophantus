\section{Formal verification}
\label{sec:lean}

All constructions above are accompanied by machine-checked proofs in Lean~4
\cite{lean4} (version 4.33.1). The development uses \emph{only} the Lean kernel
and its core library: no Mathlib, no other dependency. This is a deliberate
constraint. The facts we need---completeness of the Pell recursion, a
congruence, monotonicity---are all in Mathlib, and importing them would have
been faster; but the point of the exercise is that the figures should not rest
on a citation, and a proof that is one \texttt{import} away from a large library
does not make that visible.

The equisatisfiability theorems have the shape
\[
  \bigl(\exists a\,b\dots z\in\Z:\; \textstyle\bigwedge_i 0\le\cdot \;\wedge\;
  \mathrm{complete}(k,a,\dots,z)\bigr)
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \bigl(\exists a\,b\dots x:\; \textstyle\bigwedge_i 0\le\cdot \;\wedge\;
  \mathrm{reduced}(k,a,\dots,x)\bigr) .
\]
We work over $\Z$ with nonnegativity as separate hypotheses rather than over
$\N$. The reason is that truncated subtraction on $\N$ ($3-5=0$) would silently
change the meaning of every equation containing a minus sign; stating the same
equations over $\Z$ and carrying $0\le x$ as hypotheses says what we mean.

\subsection{Compiling is not the same as stating}

A formalization guarantees the \emph{proof}; it says nothing about whether the
\emph{statement} is the intended one. This is not a hypothetical concern. Our
generator of \texttt{Aplanado.lean} produced, in succession, three defective
statements, two of which compiled:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item eliminated unknowns \emph{quantified} instead of substituted, so the
        theorem asserted something weaker;
  \item the hypothesis named \texttt{h}, shadowing the unknown $h$ of
        \cite{jsww1976};
  \item a defining equation with a negative coefficient emitted over $\N$.
\end{itemize}

We therefore treat the statement as an artifact to be checked in its own right,
by two mechanisms.

\paragraph{Generation, not transcription.}
\texttt{Aplanado.lean} is emitted by the same program that computes the figure,
from the same in-memory object. A sign copied wrongly would give a file that
compiles and means something else; generating it removes the copying step. The
same discipline produces the \LaTeX{} of the system displayed in
\S\ref{ssec:flat-system}.

\paragraph{Statement tests.}
For each Lean file there is a test that parses the \emph{text} of the file,
translates its hypotheses back into polynomials, and checks with a computer
algebra system that they are, one by one and positionally, the published
equations; that the eliminated unknowns appear substituted rather than
quantified; and that un-naming the flattened system---replacing each $\mu_i$ by
its definition to a fixed point---returns the original system exactly, with
nothing missing and nothing extra. These tests are what would catch a theorem
that compiles and says the wrong thing.

\subsection{Axioms}

Every theorem in the development was audited with \verb|#print axioms|. All of
them depend on at most \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and
\texttt{Quot.sound}---the three axioms of the Lean kernel---and none on
\texttt{sorryAx}. There are no \texttt{axiom} declarations of our own and no
\texttt{native\_decide}. The audit is part of the build script, so a regression
would fail the build rather than pass silently.
